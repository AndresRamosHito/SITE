Introduction

Aim and scope

This manual describes how to decide, mount, capture and assemble a Lankester Composite Digital Plate (LCDP) for Orchidaceae whose morphology is hard to record: minute flowers, three-dimensional structures, glossy or translucent surfaces, and information hidden inside the flower. It combines a decision method (what must be documented and why) with equipment and capture protocols (how to do it so that another team can repeat it).

Who it is for

Students, technicians and researchers preparing plates for taxonomic descriptions, revisions, collection databases or conservation material. It assumes basic familiarity with orchid floral morphology and with using a camera in manual mode.

Part I

Principles

1.1 The LCDP as a scientific document

An LCDP is a plate that brings together, in a single composition, images taken at different scales, from different orientations and with different techniques (macro photography, focus stacking, photomicrography, dissection) to communicate the diagnostic morphology of a species (Karremans, 2020). Its value lies not in each photograph on its own, but in how the whole organises the information: what is shown, at what scale and in relation to what.

An LCDP should therefore be treated as evidence, not as illustration. It must let another researcher check a character without the specimen in hand, and it must be linkable to a voucher deposited in a collection. LCDPs originated at the Lankester Botanical Garden (University of Costa Rica) as part of its orchid documentation protocols. Karremans made the method available in an open-access document, LCDP – 2019, which covers the basic equipment, the elements to photograph, the editing rules and the publication format (Karremans, 2020).

Composite digital documentation is no longer limited to orchids. The same techniques, above all focus stacking and image stitching, are applied to other plant groups and to animals such as insects (Verma and Saxena, 2025), and the Lankester protocol is one of the forerunners of that work.

1.2 The loss of morphological information

Every image turns a three-dimensional, living and often minute structure into a two-dimensional record under particular conditions of orientation, scale, focus and lighting. Something is always lost in that transformation. This manual calls loss of morphological information the reduction in a representation's ability to preserve one or more characters present in the organism.

Loss is not necessarily a technical error. A frontal view may record the colour and shape of the perianth well and still lose the depth of a mentum; an image of the whole flower may lose the ornamentation of the callus. Documenting therefore involves three operations:

1. Identify what information is at risk of being lost in this specimen.
2. Decide whether that information has diagnostic value.
3. Reduce the loss with the minimum sufficient strategy.

The causes of loss fall into five limitations (size, three-dimensional form, optical properties, access to internal information and physical properties), which organise the diagnosis in Part III.

1.3 Six principles

PrincipleWhat it requiresPractical consequence
FidelityRepresent the characters as they are in the specimen.Editing improves legibility but never reconstructs, removes damage or alters characters (see 5.4).
CompletenessRecord all the information relevant to the aim of the study.If one image is not enough, the missing representation is added; the character is not omitted.
Morphological contextPreserve the spatial relationships between structures.Every isolated detail must be locatable in the whole flower.
ReproducibilityAnother team can repeat the record.Equipment, mounting, lighting and parameters are documented for each panel (Appendix B).
RelevanceChoose the technique for the specimen, not out of habit.There is no standard plate with a fixed number of panels.
TransparencyEvery decision is explicit and justifiable.The limitations of the material and the processing steps are recorded.

1.4 The decision rule

Single rule

Every panel on the plate answers an information need that the existing representation does not cover.

Everything else follows from this rule:

· A plate is judged by the information it preserves, not by how it looks.
· Planning starts from the characters that must be shown, not from the equipment available.
· An extra view that repeats existing information is redundant, even if it is a good photograph.
· The same species may need different plates if the aim of the study changes.

Lepanthes in this part

Lepanthes is an extreme case of information loss. The flowers are often a few millimetres across, the petals are transversely bilobed and the bilaminate lip clasps the column and hides part of it. A single frontal view loses scale, depth and the lip–column relationship at once, and that relationship is often diagnostic. This is why the genus runs through the whole manual. The worked example is Lepanthes sp. nov., from the Rufford project «El Oro del Cofre».

Part II

Anatomy of an LCDP

2.1 Canonical panels

The base representation of an LCDP is the set of panels against which missing information is assessed. Not all are compulsory; the table shows when each is included.

Flower, lateral viewProfile of the flower: ovary, mentum, position of the lip.Almost always; required if there is a mentum, spur or hinged lip.

2.2 Template

The Lankester guide (Karremans, 2020) sets the starting format: letter-size plate (215.9 × 279.4 mm), black background, at least 300 ppi and 16-bit RGB.

The final format depends on the journal. In Species Orchidacearum, for example, only horizontal scales are used, one under each element, white, 6 px thick with two 10 px vertical end bars. The measurement is in white Arial 12 pt, centred 20 px above the scale, with number and unit separated by a space (for example, «5 mm»). Each element carries a capital letter in Arial 14 pt by its lower corner, in the order of the botanical description (2.3).

2.3 Scales, labelling and plate caption

· Each panel has its own scale bar, calibrated for its magnification. A shared scale is only valid if the panels have exactly the same magnification.
· Panel letters follow the order of the botanical description: plant, inflorescence, flower, perianth, lip, column, anther cap and pollinarium.
· Scale values must be comparable at a glance: 1, 3, 5 or 10 mm, with no decimals and no uninformative values such as 7, 9 or 11. Horizontal and vertical scales are not mixed, and slanted scales are never used (Karremans, 2020).
· The plate caption includes: scientific name with author, voucher (collector and number), collection or herbarium of deposit, authorship of the photographs and of the composition, and the identification of each panel with its scale value.

2.4 Metadata and voucher

Each LCDP is linked to a voucher. Lankester records come almost entirely from wild material with locality data, and the plate must keep that traceability.

FieldNotes
Taxon and determinerAccepted name, author and who determined it.
Collector and numberAs written on the voucher.
Locality and coordinatesFor threatened or much-collected species, publish generalised coordinates and keep the exact ones in the internal record.
DepositHerbarium, living collection or spirit material; accession number.

Part III

Diagnosis and decision tree

3.1 State of the specimen

The first decision is whether the material is usable. The quality of the plate depends more on the flower chosen than on any later technique.

CheckIf it fails
Full anthesis, no wilting or dehydrationChoose another flower; if buds are about to open, wait.
Floral parts intact, no insect or handling damageChoose another flower; if there is none, document it and record the damage in the caption.
Pollinarium presentLook for an unvisited flower; record its absence if there is none.
Natural orientation preservedPhotograph the flower in situ before cutting it.

Digital editing is no substitute for a good flower: it is not used to rebuild parts, hide damage or correct deformities.

3.2 Is the base representation enough?

With the base panels of 2.1 defined, list the diagnostic characters of the group and check, one by one, whether each character is clearly visible in some panel. Only the characters that are not visible lead to an additional decision. This check is the entry point of the tree (3.5).

Diagnostic value is assessed at the start, not at the end: a character with no value for distinguishing or characterising the taxon does not justify an extra panel, however hard it is to see.

3.3 The five limitations

When a diagnostic character is not visible, the cause fits one or more of these limitations. They are not mutually exclusive.

LimitationSigns in the specimenCheck questionResponse
SizeMinute structures, fine ornamentation, trichomes.Does the character lose definition at the scale of the panel?Detail at higher magnification; photomicrography beyond the macro range.
3D formVolume, curvature, cavities, parts on different planes.Can one view convey depth, orientation and the relationship between parts?Complementary view; 3D model if several views are not enough.
Optical propertiesGloss, white parts, saturated colours, shallow depth of field.Is the character visible to the eye but not in the image?Improve the record: light, polarisation, exposure, colour, stacking.
Internal accessCavities, parts covering others, internal structures.Is the character invisible from outside?First another orientation; if that is not enough, dissection, section or cut.
Physical propertiesThin, translucent or fragile parts, or parts that dehydrate quickly.Does the structure change during preparation or capture?Early capture, humidity control, transmitted light, dissection order.

Tree by limitation

Size only counts if the character loses definition at the scale of the panel and has diagnostic value.

A view is added only if it shows depth, relief or orientation that the main view does not.

The information is in the flower but does not reach the image: correct the capture, not the specimen.

Before dissecting or cutting, always try an alternative orientation.

Delicacy, thinness, translucency or dehydration matter if they change the structure during capture.

Two rules prevent the most common mistakes. First: a limitation only counts if it affects a diagnostic character. Second: before intervening on the specimen (dissecting or cutting), always try an alternative orientation.

    4.2 Cameras and optics

    Case study: OM System OM-1 Mark II camera with a Laowa 60 mm 2x macro lens and Canon–OM adapter, giving just over 2x, on a home-built focusing rail.

    Record the real step of the rail. In the case study the step estimated by the operator (0.4 to 0.6 mm per frame) did not match the one derived from fitting the views together (between 0.12 and 0.24 mm), and forcing the estimated value multiplied the error by 10 to 20 times. To fix the scale, photograph a stage micrometer or a scale bar at the same magnification.

    4.5 Preparing and mounting the specimen

    If a 3D model is planned, the mount must let the flower turn without being taken down. In the case study the flower was remounted between views, so each view has an independent pose and could only be registered with anatomical landmarks; any movement of sepals, petals or lip blades between mounts mixes with the registration error. If the support does not rotate, include a small rigid reference, such as a pin head, in every view.

    5.2 Focus stacking

    Software in the case study: Helicon Focus 8.3.11, method C. From each stack the clean fused image is kept; it serves as the texture and the pixel reference for that view.

    If there will be a 3D model, the aligned layers are also exported (RGBA PNG, one per frame, about 5066 × 3840 px). The alpha channel marks the in-focus region Helicon chose. All 3D geometry is built from these layers, not from the OBJ of Helicon's 3D viewer, which in the case study was a 256 × 256 height map with inverted depth and a flipped, watermarked texture.

    Helicon prints its watermark on the last layer of each stack, in colour and in alpha, so that layer is excluded. Also check the direction of the stack: in the case study the exported layers ran from far to near, the reverse of the shooting order. Where parts overlap, the part in front must have the frame index closest to the lens.

    5.3 Photomicrography

    Verma and Saxena (2025) describe a workflow that suits structures needing a microscope: a DSLR mounted on a trinocular microscope with 4x, 10x and 20x plan achromat objectives, cable release to avoid vibration, and a flash with diffuser. The focus step is calculated from the depth of field of each objective: 1 to 2 µm at 20x, and 5 to 10 µm at 4x and 10x. When the structure does not fit in one field, overlapping fields are taken, each is stacked, and they are then stitched into a mosaic.

    5.4 Editing: what is allowed and what is not

    AllowedNot allowed
    White balance and colour correction with a reference chartChanging the colour of a structure
    Exposure, contrast and curves applied to the whole imageLocal adjustments that hide or create relief
    Cropping and rotating to orient the panelDistorting or scaling along one axis only
    Removing dust or fibres from the background, and accidental stains or damage (for example, on the leaves) when it is a correction that does not alter the original morphologyRemoving trichomes, pollen or any structure belonging to the specimen, or adding elements not observed in that individual

    5.5 Assembling and exporting the plate

    This is how plates are assembled at Lankester today, in Adobe Photoshop (Karremans, 2020). An LCDP is more than a set of photographs: it follows a visual logic that starts at capture and must give a balanced, informative result.

    1. File. Create a file in the format of 2.2 and work on it as a PSD, with each element on its own layer.
    2. Originals. Each photograph is first developed from RAW (NEF, DNG or similar), adjusting brightness, contrast and colour temperature. Each element is photographed next to a measurement reference that is later replaced by the scale bar.
    3. Layout. Follow the reading order, left to right and top to bottom. The flower, the most important element, goes in the top left corner; the habit closes the plate in the bottom right corner (top right if the plant is pendent), and the dissected perianth usually goes at the bottom. The other elements fill the free space, as each specimen allows. Plates must be comparable across species: the same elements, photographed the same way and in equivalent positions.
    4. Orientation. Natural orientation is respected (pendent plants, non-resupinate flowers), each element sits straight vertically or horizontally, and all face the inside of the plate.
    5. Sizes. What is small in reality must not outgrow what is large: anther and pollinarium are enlarged, but never beyond the habit or the flower, and the habit, though reduced, remains one of the dominant elements.
    6. Space. The plate should be full, without large gaps. Elements may overlap with a shadow if no information is lost.
    7. Scales and letters. Placed according to 2.2 and 2.3, close to their element and aligned with each other.

    Part VI

    Three-dimensional documentation

    This part summarises the three-dimensional reconstruction method developed with Lepanthes sp. nov. in November 2026. It is provisional and describes the state of model v9.

    6.1 When 3D is justified

    A three-dimensional model answers the 3D-form limitation when complementary views do not resolve the spatial relationship between parts, or when the number of views needed would make the plate unreadable. Like any panel, it is justified by the information it adds, not by the technique.

    6.2 The Lepanthes 3D workflow

    The aim is a solid, complete and metric surface model of a flower a few millimetres across, built from focus stacks taken from three angles: lateral, frontal and ventral. The work follows three stages in a fixed order: smooth, complete geometry without texture; geometric surface detail (papillae on petals and lip, scaly relief on sepals); and colour texture. In the case study the first stage is finished and the other two are planned.

    The geometry is built from the lateral and frontal views. The ventral view provides colour only, because its depth proved internally inconsistent: the anther ended up displaced by 1.85 mm and no single depth scale fitted the view.

    6.3 Integrating 3D into the LCDP

    Available outputs: full model and light model (400,000 triangles) in GLB format, in millimetres. They open in Blender with File › Import › glTF 2.0.

    6.4 Limitations of the case study

    Scale. Absolute scale was fixed by convention: 10 mm between the apex of the dorsal sepal and the midpoint of the lateral sepal apices. At exactly 2x, the optics would put that distance at about 7.6 mm, so the model may be oversized by around 30 %.

    Remounting. The flower was remounted between views. Any change of shape between mounts is absorbed into the alignment error and the symmetry mismatch, and has not been measured separately. Recommendations to avoid it are in 4.2 and 4.5.

    Lip. With two views, the lip solid comes out box-like. The next step is to use the ventral outline as a third carving view.

    Margins. About 3 % of the margin still exceeds the curvature law, and some 8,600 open edges remain, mostly along thin margins where only one face was reconstructed.

    Case study: Lepanthes

    7.1 Why Lepanthes

    Worked example: Lepanthes sp. nov., Rufford project «El Oro del Cofre», November 2026. The genus brings together all five limitations of Part III: flowers a few millimetres across, bilobed petals, a bilaminate lip that clasps the column, parts that dehydrate soon after cutting, and surface papillae that, at this magnification, dominate the depth signal.
