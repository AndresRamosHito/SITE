Research / Pollination mechanics

When pollen stays behind.

A pollinator must leave its pollen in the flower. In controlled Cattleya walkeriana trials, withdrawal rate changed where the connection failed—and whether the stigma kept the pollinia.

Andres E. Ramos R. & Tetsuo Yamaguchi
Orchidarc / University of Tokyo

Submitted to Botanical Journal of the Linnean SocietyOrchidaceae Special Issue · Under consideration

This research summary includes the 8 October 2026 video reanalysis and proposed corrected figures. Peer review is ongoing.

01 / The biological question

What gives way when the pollinator leaves?

Four pollinia—the orchid’s pollen masses—sit between the withdrawing mimic and the receiving stigma. Their caudicles connect them to the rostellar adhesive, or viscarium. Deposition depends on which link separates first.

10, 30 & 50 mm/min · Three clean trials

The stigma’s composite gives way.

The cellular matrix stretches and fails internally. All four pollinia leave with the mimic in each trial.

0/12 pollinia retained in the stigma
100 & 150 mm/min · Two clean trials

The caudicle–pollinium junction releases.

The stretched connectors release while most pollinia remain in the stigmatic cavity: three at 100 mm/min and four at 150 mm/min.

7/8 pollinia retained in the stigma
An observed switch, with limited scope

The clean trials bracket a change between 50 and 100 mm/min. They do not establish a species-wide threshold or a natural pollinator’s departure speed. Across the full dataset, mixed contact and slipping also affect the outcome.

03 / Match the force to the event

The highest force comes before release.

The October reanalysis separates the initial engagement peak from the later event that decides deposition. In four of the five clean trials, the peak occurs within the first millimetre of crosshead travel, before visible failure.

For the two clean junction-release trials, the early peaks are 0.257 and 0.235 N. Force just before the first caudicle–pollinium release is lower: 0.095 and 0.116 N, at 100 and 150 mm/min respectively.

These are forces measured across the loaded system. Two trials cannot establish a rate-independent breaking force. The three cohesive-failure trials are also too few to establish a general material law.

04 / Evidence & scope

A mechanical experiment with a biological outcome.

What was measured

  • A resin pollinator mimic was withdrawn from the stigmatic cavity at controlled crosshead rates.
  • Force records and lateral video identify contact, stretching, release and pollinia retention.
  • Five trials were selected for usable contact and a single dominant failure pathway; all 20 trials are reported.

What the sample can support

  • A descriptive association between withdrawal rate, failure pathway and pollen retention in the selected trials.
  • Direct evidence that the early force peak and later release are distinct events.
  • A testable mechanism for further experiments with standardized contact and more independent flowers.

The 20 tests are mechanical trials on commercially grown flowers. Genet identity and flower age were not recorded, and some material was reused sequentially. Selection of the five clean trials depends partly on the observed outcome. Trial 09 has about 11 seconds of initial slip before sustained matrix loading; trial 01 has ten stills rather than a video. Crosshead rate is an apparatus setting and cannot be equated directly with an animal’s flight speed.

Why the caudicle estimates remain exploratory

Apparent stiffness and extension depend on the assumed number, length and cross-section of loaded caudicles. Crosshead displacement also includes deformation and slip elsewhere in the assembly. These are system-level estimates, so they do not establish intrinsic caudicle constants or a conserved mechanism across orchid genera. The exploratory Cymbidium controls are not used here to infer a rostellum function.
