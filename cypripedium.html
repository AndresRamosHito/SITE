Veracruz · Cypripedium sect. Irapeana · Vol. IV · MMXXVI

The golden slippers of Veracruz

The largest known population of Mexican lady’s slipper orchids grows on a single mountain in central Veracruz. This is what three visits in 2026 taught us, and what now threatens it.

How it began

A single photograph, then a mountain of gold

In the summer of 2023 a photograph shared by naturalist Gabriel on the forum of the Asociación Mexicana de Orquideología led us to Veracruz. Across the slopes of one mountain we found a vast population of golden-flowered Cypripedium, the lady’s slippers of section Irapeana, one of the oldest lineages in the genus. We now estimate around 10,000 shoots across the site. Connected shoots can share one rhizome, so this is an estimate of shoots, not of separate plants, but it still dwarfs most known colonies.

Section Irapeana is usually split into three species: the large, widespread C. irapeanum, the smaller C. molle of Oaxaca and Puebla, and the tiny C. dickinsonianum of Chiapas. On this one mountain we find plants that look like all three. All of them are terrestrial, die back to an underground rhizome in the dry season, and depend on soil fungi, which is why they have never thrived in cultivation.

In the spring of 2024, wildfires of a scale never recorded before swept central Veracruz, including these slopes. The plants above ground burned, but many rhizomes survived, and in 2025 the grasslands came back with dense, flowering clumps. We keep the exact locality private to protect the population.

Shoots appear with the rains, the first flowers open in early to mid July, and the last fade in mid to late September. In the dry months the plants retreat to their rhizomes underground.

What we learned in 2026

Habitat does not decide the size of a plant

Our first idea was simple: each habitat had its own growth form. Three visits this season showed that the picture is far less tidy.

2023 idea · largely disproven

One habitat, one form

  • Tall, single-stemmed giants with the largest flowers in the shade of oaks.
  • Clumps of shorter stems in the open grassland.
  • Compact dwarfs in shallow, rocky soil at the summit.
2026 field result

Size crosses habitats

  • The biggest plants are still mostly in the oak forest, but we also found big plants growing in the grassland.
  • Robust and compact plants grow close together, so plant size is not a reliable sign of habitat or of species.
  • We found a fourth sub-habitat we had not recognised before: the encinillo, a low scrub of shrubby oak.
One site, four sub-habitats

Oak, grass, rock and encinillo

Oak forest

Home to most of the largest plants, single stems close to a metre tall with flowers up to 12 cm across.

Grassland

Clumps of six or more stems turn the meadows gold. In 2026 we also found big plants here.

Rocky summit

Compact plants in shallow dark and red soils, sharing the slope with yellow-and-red Bletia.

New in 2026

Encinillo

A bushy forest of Quercus microphylla only about 50 cm tall. The orchids flower just above its canopy, and both the shrub oak and the orchids appear to withstand fire.

Threat · conservation status

Healthy, but under threat

I

Charcoal production

The oak forest is being cut down and burned to make charcoal. This is the biggest current threat to the population, and one we had not documented before this year. The oak forest holds most of the largest plants.

II

Loss of epiphytes

The oaks have lost most of their epiphytes. Orchids such as Prosthechea, Encyclia and Epidendrum once covered their branches. Without them the forest is drier and more open, which can change the pollinators, soils and moisture the slipper orchids rely on.

III

Fire

The rhizomes survive burns, and the encinillo and grassland plants seem fire-tolerant. Repeated or hotter fires, together with grazing and clearing, can still push the population past recovery.

Our assessment, 2026

The population looks healthy, flowering in its thousands, but it is definitely under threat.

What we are doing

Eyes on the mountain all year

Monitoring

Two local volunteers

A second volunteer joined us this year. Together they will survey the site at least once every two months, through the flowering season and the dry months.

Seed

Seed collection

The volunteers will collect seed. Seed from our first season, germinated at the University of Sheffield by Orchidarc trustee Dr David Gregory, has already produced seedlings in the laboratory.

Next

Genetic analyses

We will sequence plants to confirm which species grow here and to understand how this population has evolved. Earlier sequences from the site disagree between markers, which may point to gene flow between lineages.

VIII Mexican Orchidology Meeting · Xalapa · 2026

Thousands of plants, hundreds of forms

We presented the photographic record from the site at the VIII Encuentro Mexicano de Orquideología, with Miguel A. Lozano Rodriguez (Universidad Veracruzana) and Julián Duarte (AMO Herbarium).

Main findings
  • Size does not fall into discrete categories. A small plant cannot be assigned to a small species on size alone.
  • Some flower characters, such as the lip, the windows in the pouch and the staminode, show patterns worth measuring against C. irapeanum, C. molle and C. dickinsonianum.
  • Sanger sequences made at Sheffield in 2024 give an irapeanum-like signal in ITS and matK and a molle-like signal in Xdh. That fits introgression or incomplete lineage sorting, but does not prove a hybrid origin.

This is an exploratory photographic record. The next step is measured, voucher-linked work under permit.
