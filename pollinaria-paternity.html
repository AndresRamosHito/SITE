Orchid paternity
& the genetic funnel

One capsule can hold thousands of seeds, all sharing a father. When only a few fruits supply the next generation, a population of many orchids can become a population of few fathers.

The question

Which fathers reach
the next generation?

Pollinaria package pollen into a small number of transfers. In the hard-pollinium orchids studied so far, capsule progeny have been recovered as single paternal sibships. A fruit with one sire can still belong to a mother that mates with several donors across different flowers.

The population consequence depends on recruitment. If a successful fruit contributes many siblings, its father receives a large share of the next generation. This study combines primary paternity evidence with a diploid individual-based model to examine that genetic funnel.

01 / Primary-study evidence

What the capsules tell us

Single paternity is a finding in particular sampled systems. Pollen-unit architecture provides biological context; it does not make every orchid fruit single sired.

Hard pollinia

Three species.
477 capsule arrays.

Laelia rubescens, Phalaenopsis pulcherrima and Rhynchostylis gigantea yielded single paternal sibships in the studies assembled in the manuscript.

Only 18 arrays used microsatellites or SNPs; 459 used allozymes. Rare mixed capsules and small secondary sires can escape detection.

Soft pollinia

Mixing remains
possible.

In Chiloglottis, 27% and 41% of capsules were multiply sired in the two studied taxa. Sectile pollinia can also deliver massulae from several donors to a stigma.

Detected father counts, correlated paternity and effective sire numbers describe different quantities. They are kept separate.

The sampling limit

A minor father
can be missed.

A sire contributing 10% of the seeds has a 35% chance of going undetected in a random sample of ten seedlings.

0.910 ≈ 0.35

This is a sampling calculation, not an estimate of hidden mixed paternity in orchids.

Access to fruits.
Packaging of siblings.

The first term captures inequality in flowering and siring opportunities. The second combines retained recruits per establishing fruit with within-fruit paternal identity. In a closed population at carrying capacity, r̄ ≈ 1/(φLfq): the paternity effect becomes larger as fruit set falls.

What if establishment is independent? At 25% fruit set, the strict-paternity reduction in Me falls from 18.5% with the shared gate to 9.0% with independent establishment. At 2.5% fruit set, the reduction remains 44–50% across the tested correlations.

What if genes arrive from outside? External paternity and adult immigration raise diversity and compress its absolute difference between regimes. They do not remove the lower effective number of resident male contributors. The higher-input setting also changes donor evenness; separate factorial runs distinguish these effects.

The model

Diploid adults, paternal shares within fruits, neutral inheritance, establishment and density regulation. Fifty discrete, non-overlapping generations. Outcrossing only. External sires and adult immigrants enter at different stages.

Me describes resident male contribution. NeH estimates inbreeding effective size from diversity decay in closed populations. H is a simulated neutral marker, not an empirical estimate of orchid genomic diversity.

The limits

The simulations omit adult survival across episodes, clonality, dormancy and seed banks. They do not simulate pollen transport, geographic distance, heritable traits or speciation. The shared establishment gate is a limiting case; partial and independent establishment are tested separately.

The milkweed comparison has different demographic parameters. It illustrates their importance and does not provide a matched test of an intrinsic orchid–milkweed difference.

The next field test

Genotype capsule progeny and natural recruitment cohorts around mapped mothers. Measure how many siblings from each fruit enter the next generation, alongside fruit set, census size and lifetime reproductive success.

The proposed link between precise pollinarium placement, adhesive properties and diversification is a hypothesis. It requires direct mechanical and progeny-based tests.
