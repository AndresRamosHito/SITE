Research

The science behind the conservation.

Orchidarc is a research-led organisation. Three active programmes sit behind our fieldwork: the RAR Climate Replicator for habitat simulation, a biomaterials programme on orchid pollination adhesives, and field-based work on micro-endemic Lepanthes in the Cofre de Perote cloud forests.

§ 01

RAR Climate Replicator.

A modular automated greenhouse system developed to simulate orchid habitats for research, conservation and cultivation.

The RAR Climate Replicator is an automated habitat-replication system built in-house by Orchidarc. By integrating sensors, programmable climate control, LED lighting, automated watering and remote monitoring, it recreates the temperature, humidity, airflow and water conditions required by sensitive orchid species.

What it does

At its core, the system is a controlled micro-greenhouse designed specifically to reproduce the environmental conditions of natural orchid habitats, not the homogenised conditions of a standard nursery.

  • Climate control. Precise regulation of temperature, humidity and airflow, matched to target habitats.
  • Lighting simulation. Programmable LEDs reproduce natural light cycles, including sunrise and sunset transitions.
  • Automated watering. Pumps and valves run on scheduled, sensor-triggered, or habitat-specific programmes.
  • Habitat replication. Configurable for cloud forest microclimates, flowing-water habitats, or limestone seepage systems.
  • Remote monitoring. The system transmits data for real-time monitoring and remote control.

Why it matters for conservation

The biggest bottleneck in laboratory-based orchid conservation is deflasking: the moment an in-vitro seedling leaves its sterile flask and has to survive in the open air. The chamber creates a gradient between sterile culture, controlled acclimatisation and eventual greenhouse or field conditions.

§ 02

The viscidium.

An orchid bioadhesive, the size of a sesame seed, that holds together one of evolution's most elaborate pollination systems. The subject of our PhD research at the University of Tokyo.

Orchid pollination does not rely on loose pollen. Each flower packs its pollen into waxy pollinia that must be transferred whole, by a pollinator, to the stigma of another flower. Holding this system together is a tiny specialised glue: the viscidium.

What the viscidium does

When a pollinator brushes past, the viscidium attaches the pollinia to the pollinator's body. Later, when the pollinator visits another flower, the pollinia are deposited on a receptive stigmatic surface.

Our research

Orchidarc's doctoral research at the University of Tokyo, in partnership with the University of Sheffield, studies the viscidium and related stigmatic gels across orchid genera. The goal is to understand an under-studied piece of orchid biology and characterise a potentially novel class of bio-inspired adhesives.

This is where conservation and materials science feed each other directly: the species we study are also the species we protect in the field.

§ 03

Lepanthes micro-endemics.

Population mapping, macro documentation and genetic sampling for miniature cloud-forest orchids from the Cofre de Perote foothills.

The Cofre de Perote foothills hold recently described and potentially undescribed Lepanthes populations. Many are miniature epiphytes restricted to narrow cloud-forest microhabitats, so taxonomic resolution and locality-level monitoring are urgent.

Research focus

Orchidarc is combining repeated field surveys, macro photography, GPS mapping, host-tree notes and silica-gel leaf sampling to clarify species boundaries and estimate Area of Occupancy for these populations.

Why it matters

Some known populations may be restricted to single forest fragments. A single clearing event, wildfire or land-use decision could remove a substantial part of a species' global population, making careful documentation a prerequisite for conservation action.
